# Why Causality Matters

Today, it’s common to hear phrases like:

> “Let me ask ChatGPT.”

Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and LLaMA can generate remarkably useful responses by training on massive amounts of internet-scale text data.

However, despite their impressive capabilities, these models still face major limitations when reasoning about cause-and-effect relationships.

This distinction is important because:

- Correlation does not imply causation
- Statistical association is not the same as understanding mechanisms
- Real-world systems often require causal reasoning rather than pattern matching

---

# The Causal Hierarchy

One of the most influential frameworks for understanding causality comes from Judea Pearl’s work on causal inference.

Pearl describes causal reasoning as a hierarchy with three levels:

## 1. Association

The model recognizes patterns and correlations in data.

Example:

- Ice cream sales increase when drowning incidents increase.

An LLM is generally very strong at this level because training data is fundamentally statistical.

---

## 2. Intervention

The model reasons about what happens when we actively change something.

Example:

- What happens to production quality if we change a manufacturing process?

This requires understanding the effect of actions rather than simply observing correlations.

---

## 3. Counterfactual Reasoning

The highest level of causal reasoning.

Example:

- Would the accident still have happened if the machine had been maintained properly?

This involves reasoning about alternative realities and hypothetical outcomes.

---

# Where LLMs Perform Well — and Where They Struggle

Recent research suggests that modern LLMs perform very well on association-based tasks but struggle significantly when deeper causal reasoning is required.

Some important studies include:

- *Language Models as Causal Reasoners* (Kıcıman et al., 2023)
- *Causal Reasoning in Large Language Models* (Jin et al., 2024)

The common finding across many experiments is:

- LLMs are excellent at recognizing patterns
- Performance drops on intervention tasks
- Performance drops even further on counterfactual reasoning

One possible reason is how these systems are trained.

Most modern LLMs use next-token prediction objectives, meaning they learn to predict the next word in a sequence. While this allows them to capture extremely rich statistical relationships, it does not necessarily force them to learn underlying causal mechanisms.

This idea is also discussed in research on causal representation learning by Yoshua Bengio and others.

# My Initial Experiment

To explore this idea myself, I ran some preliminary experiments using a LLaMA-based model on a local machine with:

- RTX 4070 Super GPU
- Ryzen 7800X3D CPU

The goal was simple:

> Test whether the model could move beyond association-based reasoning.

## Initial Observation

Without structured prompting:

- The model performed well on correlation-style questions
- The model struggled more on intervention and counterfactual reasoning tasks

This behavior aligns closely with findings reported in benchmark studies such as:

- BIG-bench
- Causal QA datasets
- Recent LLM reasoning evaluations

Causal reasoning is deeply connected to many industrial engineering problems, including:

- Quality control
- Process optimization
- Supply chain management
- Reliability engineeringa
- Manufacturing systems
- Decision-making under uncertainty
